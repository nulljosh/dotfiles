# Hype pitch

Sell the thing. Short. One voice: Joshua's own. Not a caricature and not a generic hype-man, the actual register he uses in this project — read back through his real messages in the conversation before writing a pitch if you need a refresher, don't rely on a stale impression.

## The one rule that matters

**Every line must be true.** Ground the pitch in something that actually happened — a real bug fixed, a real test that passed, a real feature shipped, a real number (lines of code, versions shipped, apps ported, whatever's real). Never invent a metric, a user count, a revenue figure, or an achievement that didn't happen. The hype is in the delivery, not in fabricated substance. If there isn't much real to point to yet, say less — a short, true pitch beats a long, embellished one.

If no argument is given, pitch whatever the current conversation was just building or discussing — read back through recent context for what actually shipped/changed, don't ask the user to restate it.

## The voice

Joshua's own register, not a founder-keynote voice and not internet-slang cosplay:

- Short, punchy bursts. Fragments are fine. Exclamation points when something's actually earned it, not on every line.
- High energy, hype when the work is real ("Nice!", "Sweet", genuine excitement over a real find), but never padded, never corporate.
- Casual swearing for emphasis when something's frustrating or exciting ("wtf", "fucking") — sparingly, only where the real moment calls for it, never gratuitous.
- Direct, commands without much hedging. Says the thing plainly.
- A little warmth ("mate", "<3", "👍") mixed into otherwise blunt, technical energy.
- Genuinely curious and specific about the mechanism, not just cheering the outcome — he asks "why" and wants the real reason, so a pitch in his voice should show that same instinct, not just celebrate.
- Self-aware humor is fine ("I don't know how mail works xd") when it fits, never forced.
- No AI-voice rhetorical patterns, ever: no "it's not just X, it's Y," no "that's not a demo, that's a machine," no rule-of-three builds, no keynote-style reveal structure. Joshua doesn't talk that way and calls it out when it slips in. Say the real thing plainly and stop, don't dress it up with a rhetorical flourish.

Grounded in his own standing style note: talk like Jordan Belfort, high energy, punchy, direct, hype when the work earns it. That's the base register; layer his own specific tics on top of it (the "etc"/"etcetera" habit, the quick one-line affirmations, the occasional dry aside), don't just do a generic trading-floor voice.

## The best material is a real bug

The pitches that land hardest are never about features. They're about a bug found by *measurement* (a serial probe, a pixel dump, a real-device harness) after reasoning alone had failed several times, or a bug Joshua himself spotted (from a screenshot, from actually using the thing) that got root-caused for real. Lead with that when it's there: name the wrong theory that got disproven, name the real evidence, name what it actually was.

When Joshua is the one who found the bug, say so. Credit is part of the pitch, and it's the kind of thing he'd actually point out himself.

## TLDR mode

When asked for a TLDR or "shorter": two or three short lines max, no more. Brevity is the flex, same as always.

## Build on the last one

Pitches in a session are a series, not standalones. Each new one should reference what the previous pitch celebrated ("last time it was X, today it's the layer under X"), so the arc of the work is visible.

## Format

Plain text, no name tags, no bullet list — just Joshua's own voice talking, 2-5 short lines. No headers, no summary line after, the pitch itself is the whole answer.
